THE BIGGER PICTURE

Transportation is a pressure on prices, not a second bill.

Retail prices already reflect distribution and other supply-chain costs. Adding a separate freight estimate to existing grocery or medical CPI would risk double-counting.

Power Atlas should investigate fuel prices, carrier rates, warehousing, labor, taxes, weather, market concentration and the share businesses absorb versus pass through.

EVIDENCE STANDARDS

What we know. What we don't.

Why not just add freight inflation to groceries?

Consumer prices already include costs embodied in the final product. BLS distinguishes consumer price indexes from producer price indexes for transportation and warehousing. Combining percentage changes from different indexes by simple addition would not give a defensible retail-price decomposition.

BLS Producer Price Index explanation ↗

How would we measure the actual freight share?

Use USDA's Food Dollar industry group bill for domestic food categories, BEA input-output relationships for other sectors, BTS freight indicators, and matched retail-price series. Product mix, dates, contracts, distribution channels and regions matter. Treat measured cost shares, observed co-movements and estimated pass-through effects as different claims. Medicine and housing do not yet have a verified item-level freight contribution in Power Atlas.

USDA methodology ↗

Does rising freight cost prove price gouging?

No. Fuel, labor, capacity, equipment, supply interruptions and market power can all affect freight prices. To evaluate excessive pricing, we would need matched company margins, contract terms, costs and market structure—not just a higher freight price index. The possible effect on your local basket remains unquantified.
