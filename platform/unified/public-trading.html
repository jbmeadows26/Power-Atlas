Public Integrity / Reported financial interests

Who trades while governing?

A financial disclosure can show where money moved. It cannot, by itself, tell us who chose a trade, whether information was misused, or if a policy helped an investment. Start with the disclosed activity—then ask what else must be verified.

The finding: Kadoa counted 31,877 transactions in eight accounts disclosed by President Trump, versus 20,973 across Congress, for January 2025 through August 2026. The White House says outside managers run the accounts. These are not independently audited counts or evidence of wrongdoing.

The missing context matters

For the reported transaction values, Kadoa estimates broad disclosure ranges of $1.6–5.2 billion for presidential accounts and $0.6–1.8 billion for Congress. The ranges overlap. Neither represents profits.

Company counts also omit assets without tickers (including some bonds). Eight managed accounts and hundreds of congressional filers are fundamentally different reporting populations.

We cannot conclude: which trades Trump personally directed, that a particular trade used nonpublic information, or that a trade affected a policy, contract, rate or household bill.

What would be needed for a genuine conflict-of-interest investigation?

We would need to check original transaction filings and amendments, account ownership and trading discretion, material nonpublic-information access, time-stamped government actions, legal ethics standards applicable to each person, market controls, and evidence of any actual outcome. A mere trading coincidence is insufficient.
