Voting rules · explained

What ID do voters need at the polls?

States have different rules about showing identification when voting in person. Each bar shows how many states use that rule—not how many voters are affected.

Loading categories…

Why it matters: If someone lacks an accepted ID, the steps needed to have their vote counted may differ by state.

What do these categories mean?

Strict: voters without accepted ID generally must take an additional step after election day for a provisional ballot to count. Nonstrict: alternative ways to verify identity may be available. Photo/nonphoto: whether a photo is required on the ID. No ID document required: other identity checks may still apply.

Why district lines matter

Voters choose representatives within geographic districts. Changing district boundaries can change who wins—even if nobody changes their vote.

Look for: which institution draws the map, which party controls it, and whether courts have challenged it.

Did elected officials keep their promises?

Follow each specific campaign commitment from the original statement to the action taken. A promise is not fulfilled just because a related bill was introduced.

Look for: kept, partly kept, not kept, or not yet measurable—and the evidence behind each judgment.

A campaign promise meets governing reality

Why does trust in government fall?

Trust surveys show how people feel about institutions. They do not, by themselves, explain what caused those feelings.

Look for: changes over time, which institution was surveyed, and whether comparisons use the same questions.

What public trust surveys tell us
