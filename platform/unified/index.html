Voting rules · explained

What ID do voters need at the polls?

States have different rules about showing identification when voting in person. Each bar shows how many states use that rule—not how many voters are affected.

Loading categories…

Why it matters: If someone lacks an accepted ID, the steps needed to have their vote counted may differ by state.

What do these categories mean?

Strict: voters without accepted ID generally must take an additional step after election day for a provisional ballot to count. Nonstrict: alternative ways to verify identity may be available. Photo/nonphoto: whether a photo is required on the ID. No ID document required: other identity checks may still apply.

The story in 30 seconds
A campaign promise meets governing reality

A pledge is only assessable if it makes a testable commitment and someone can fairly be held responsible.

What to look for: Track the original quote, actual policy action, outcomes and institutional obstacles.

Political Promises

Track what candidates promised, which promises were independently testable, what governments actually did, and whether outcomes can fairly be attributed to the officials who made the commitments.

Promise registry

Campaign commitment, precise quotation, speaker, date, source, responsible level of government and testable target.

Denominator required

Independent adjudication

Not started, in progress, fulfilled, partially fulfilled, broken, blocked or not adjudicable, with dates and reviewer rationale.

No invented verdicts

Constraints and attribution

Congressional votes, vetoes, court decisions, fiscal limits and competing policy proposals must be accounted for before assigning responsibility.
